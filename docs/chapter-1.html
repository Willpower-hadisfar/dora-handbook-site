Scope, Definitions, and Why DORA Exists

Start with the question most practitioner guides skip: why did the EU need a new regulation for this at all? Financial entities already had operational risk frameworks, capital requirements, and sector-specific rules going back decades. DORA's recitals answer the question directly, and the answer explains almost everything that follows in this book.

Why DORA exists

The post-2008 wave of EU financial reform was built around financial resilience — capital, liquidity, prudential requirements. Digital operational resilience got comparatively little attention in that cycle, even though ICT risk is squarely a form of operational risk. By the time DORA was drafted, the EU's Single Rulebook had rules touching digital resilience and ICT security scattered across sectoral legislation, but — in the regulation's own words — not yet "fully or consistently harmonised." The practical consequence was gaps and overlaps in important areas, incident reporting chief among them, producing inconsistent national implementations of what should have been a single standard.

There's a second, more structural reason, and it's the one that explains this book's recurring argument that policy-exists isn't the same as compliant. Earlier sectoral financial legislation "often favoured a traditional quantitative approach" to risk — capital requirements, numeric thresholds, things a balance sheet can verify. ICT resilience doesn't reduce to a capital number. It needs qualitative rules covering protection, detection, recovery, and reporting — exactly the five Article 8 obligations Chapter 2 walked through, and exactly the kind of requirement that can't be satisfied by holding more capital in reserve. DORA exists because operational resilience is a different kind of problem than capital adequacy, and the EU's prior rulebook was still largely built for the capital-adequacy kind.

Who's actually in scope

Article 2(1) lists the entities bound by the Regulation, and the list is long enough that it's worth reading in full rather than assuming your own sector is obviously covered or obviously not:

credit institutions; payment institutions (including those exempted under the Payment Services Directive); account information service providers; electronic money institutions; investment firms; crypto-asset service providers and issuers of asset-referenced tokens; central securities depositories; central counterparties; trading venues; trade repositories; managers of alternative investment funds; management companies; data reporting service providers; insurance and reinsurance undertakings; insurance, reinsurance, and ancillary insurance intermediaries; institutions for occupational retirement provision; credit rating agencies; administrators of critical benchmarks; crowdfunding service providers; securitisation repositories — and, in its own category, ICT third-party service providers.

That last entry matters more than its place at the end of the list suggests. ICT third-party providers aren't financial entities, and they don't carry the full compliance burden the rest of this book has described — Chapters 2 through 6 are built for the twenty entity types above them. Their presence in Article 2(1) is what makes Chapter 7's oversight regime possible: being named in scope here is the legal hook for the Lead Overseer relationship, not an invitation to run a full ICT risk management framework of their own.

Who's carved out, and why it's not arbitrary

Article 2(3) excludes: managers of certain alternative investment funds under the AIFMD's own exemption, insurance and reinsurance undertakings falling under Solvency II's exemption, occupational retirement schemes with 15 or fewer total members, persons exempted under MiFID II, insurance intermediaries that are microenterprises or small/medium enterprises, and post office giro institutions.

None of these exclusions is a loophole. Each one tracks either an entity too small to meaningfully pose the systemic ICT risk DORA is built to manage (the 15-member pension scheme, the microenterprise insurance intermediary), or an entity already exempted from the parent directive DORA's scope is built to track (AIFMD, Solvency II, MiFID II). The proportionality thread running through Chapters 2 and 4's microenterprise carve-outs starts here, at the boundary of who's covered at all.

The two definitions everything else depends on

Article 3 defines dozens of terms, but two carry the whole rest of this book:

Digital operational resilience (Article 3(1)): "the ability of a financial entity to build, assure and review its operational integrity and reliability," covering the ICT capabilities needed to secure the network and information systems supporting continued service delivery — including during disruptions, not just in their absence. Read that last clause carefully: the definition itself assumes disruption happens and asks whether service continues through it, the same "survivable, not just preventable" framing Chapter 2's impact-tolerance discussion already assumed without spelling out where it came from.

ICT-related incident (Article 3(8)): "a single event or a series of linked events unplanned by the financial entity" that compromises network and information system security and adversely affects availability, authenticity, integrity, or confidentiality of data or services. Notice what's absent: no severity threshold. Severity — what makes an incident major rather than just an incident — is Article 18's job (Chapter 3), not this definition's. Article 3(8) draws the outer boundary of what counts as an incident at all; Article 18 decides which incidents inside that boundary actually trigger mandatory reporting.

A worked example: in scope, confirmed

Worth confirming explicitly, since every other chapter in this book has assumed it: MCC Crypto falls under Article 2(1)(f) — crypto-asset service providers — and the specific hook is its authorisation under the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation, Regulation (EU) 2023/1114. That is the first named fact the rest of this book leans on. MCC is a roughly 40-person Dublin firm, founded in 2021, granted authorisation in early 2025, running a custody-first service — client fund custody on a proprietary platform, a fiat/crypto on-ramp, and a spot execution venue. The detail that matters later: its Amsterdam branch passports that authorisation across the EU under MiCA's own regime, which is the cross-border footprint that puts it on the Central Bank of Ireland's TLPT radar in Chapter 4 and keeps its reporting anchored to a single competent authority in Chapter 3.

Note what this passage is not doing: it is not finding a gap. It is establishing that MCC is unambiguously inside the regime — the question every other chapter then treats as settled. The first question a crypto firm's board should ask isn't "how do we comply with DORA's ICT risk framework" — it's "have we confirmed our authorisation actually brings us inside Article 2(1)(f) in the first place." For MCC, the answer is yes, and the rest of this book can say how rather than whether.

What's genuinely unresolved here

- How the timing between DORA's own application date and the separate authorisation regimes referenced in Article 2(1)(f) actually played out in practice for crypto-asset service providers specifically — this book hasn't verified that sequencing against primary text and shouldn't be read as having resolved it. - Where the practical line sits for insurance intermediaries near the microenterprise/SME threshold — Article 2(3)(e)'s exclusion is a real cliff-edge, and a firm growing past that threshold needs a concrete trigger for when DORA obligations start applying, which this chapter's primary-text pass didn't surface.
